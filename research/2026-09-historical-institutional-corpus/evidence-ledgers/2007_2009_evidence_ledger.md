# Correction and Infrastructure-Rebuilding Evidence Ledger — 2007–2009

**Status:** Research synthesis. Listed claims require direct primary-source verification before publication.
**Purpose:** Preserve what the 2007–2009 correction changed in institutional posture: from growth/market-hub promises toward transparency, resilience, shared services, identity, and measurable accountability.

## Initial findings by pattern

| Pattern | Research finding | Transferable lesson | Material caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Entity and transaction visibility** | The G20, FSB, trade-repository, and later LEI/GLEIF responses are described as attempts to make entities, exposures, and transactions more visible after opacity contributed to financial-system stress. | Reusable entity identification and source/timestamped records can improve coordination and review. | Financial market infrastructure is legally and operationally distinct from a restoration or service-market system; do not call Human Blockchain a financial ledger or clearinghouse. |
| **Shared utility computing** | Cloud/SaaS, shared service, government dashboard, and security-consortium models were positioned around cost, transparency, standardization, and reduced duplication. | Start with a bounded utility and show a visible measure of value. | Centralization can create lock-in, data sovereignty, and dependency risks. |
| **Federated identity** | OpenID, OAuth, Liberty/Kantara, and public-sector identity profiles illustrate a move toward portable/federated access with formal trust frameworks. | Identity and role context need both technical interoperability and participation governance. | Friction, usability, privacy, and reliance on dominant providers can defeat a technically open identity scheme. |
| **Open data and civic infrastructure** | Open-government-data and civic-technology programs positioned accessible data and reusable public infrastructure as ways to improve transparency and innovation. | Publish usable definitions, documentation, and safe public interfaces. | Open data without rights, user need, stewardship, and sustainability can become performative transparency. |
| **Correction-era consolidation** | Shared-loss, supplier-support, public-private, and shared-service responses sought stability under stress. | A coordination layer must make ownership, risk, and incentives explicit. | Do not generalize public rescues, risk mutualization, or distressed consolidation as a business model for the Human Blockchain ecosystem. |

### Direct verification note: G20 Pittsburgh Statement

The September 24–25, 2009 G20 Pittsburgh Leaders' Statement says the group met during a transition “from crisis to recovery” and presented its response as repair of financial systems, regulatory reform, and a foundation for “strong, sustainable and balanced growth.” It stated that the G20 had designated itself the premier forum for international economic cooperation and had established the Financial Stability Board to coordinate and monitor stronger financial regulation. It also committed to improve the over-the-counter derivatives market and to create stronger tools to hold large global firms accountable for their risks. [1]

The directly transferable design lesson is not the financial program: it is the **institutional-response pattern** of making a common objective, coordinating body, assigned mandate, accountability mechanism, and implementation timetable explicit. The Human Blockchain application must remain a voluntary, lawful, market-specific coordination model—not a regulator, clearinghouse, or crisis authority.

### Direct verification note: OpenID Foundation

The current OpenID Foundation description states that it was founded in 2007 as a non-profit open standards body with a mission to lead a global community in creating identity standards that are secure, interoperable, and privacy-preserving. It describes an open, consensus-led working-group process, a contribution agreement, free specifications and conformance suites under an IP policy, participation by private entities, governments, non-profits, and individuals, and a diversified funding model of roughly membership, certification fees, and directed projects. It also describes a profile approach in which a local entity retains control over local decisions while leveraging a common standard, maintenance, testing, and cross-border interoperability support. [5]

The transferable pattern is **shared core, local profile**: stable common definitions and verification methods can coexist with jurisdiction-, industry-, or territory-specific implementation. It does not mean Human Blockchain should create a certification program or present itself as a standards body before it has governance, evidence, appeals, legal review, and demonstrated demand.

## Provisional Human Blockchain interpretation

The useful analogy is **not** “create a ledger because ledgers fix trust.” The evidence points to a more specific proposition: during a correction, systems gain legitimacy when the people, entities, data sources, permissions, decision rights, and review records are explicit enough to inspect and improve. The Human Blockchain may translate that proposition to fragmented service-market coordination only through a narrow, lawful, evidence-backed proof loop.
