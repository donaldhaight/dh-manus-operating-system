# Web 1 / Coalition Era Evidence Ledger — 1997–2000

**Status:** Research synthesis. Primary or institutional sources listed below require direct source verification before a final public historical claim is made.
**Purpose:** Preserve the difference between institutional patterns that can inform Human Blockchain and historical claims that must not be simplified into a single origin story.

## Initial findings by institutional pattern

| Pattern | Historical evidence captured | Transferable lesson | Non-transferable risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B2B market hub** | The source scan identifies commercial horizontal and vertical B2B exchanges—Commerce One, Ariba, VerticalNet, Chemdex, and related models—as central hubs promising procurement efficiency, supplier discovery, and transaction scale. It distinguishes the generic “MarketHub” label from a verified single company; the term requires follow-up. | A shared discovery/coordination layer needs a clearly defined job and a viable participation loop. | Transaction fees and valuation cannot precede working liquidity, integration, and supplier trust. |
| **Standards coalition** | W3C published XML 1.0 in February 1998; OASIS and UN/CEFACT launched ebXML work in 1999–2000; RosettaNet and other vertical groups coordinated structured business-process standards. | Collaborate on shared semantics, identity, and interoperable exchange while competing on applications. | Broad consensus can create complexity, slow release cycles, and vendor capture. |
| **Directory and identity layer** | LDAPv3, directory-enabled networking, DSML, and Windows 2000 Active Directory represent the era's attempt to make identity, policy, resources, and discovery reusable rather than application-specific. | Entity/role/permission context is foundational infrastructure, not an afterthought. | “Open” standards can still be implemented as platform lock-in; centralized identity can create privacy and control concerns. |
| **Industry consortium** | Automotive (Covisint/ANX/AIAG), high-tech (RosettaNet), construction, telecom, and industrial networks used associations, JVs, and consortia to reduce coordination costs and establish common process/data layers. | Anchor participants can solve the adoption cold start when they participate in a neutral, governed utility. | Buyer concentration can create supplier fear, antitrust risk, governance gridlock, and resistance to a centralized hub. |
| **Neutral-ground governance** | CommerceNet, W3C, OASIS, and similar forms used membership, host institutions, steering groups, working groups, and sometimes public funding to coordinate standards. | Separate shared protocol governance from commercial implementation and make decision rights explicit. | A “neutral” body needs transparent contribution, funding, decision, appeal, and IP rules to avoid capture. |

### Direct verification note: Covisint

The FTC's September 11, 2000 press release confirms that Covisint was a proposed Internet B2B exchange for automotive supply-chain product design, supply-chain management, and procurement; its intended founders were GM, Ford, DaimlerChrysler, Renault, Nissan, Commerce One, and Oracle. The FTC closed the initial formation investigation and terminated the HSR waiting period, but specifically noted that the venture was early-stage, not operational, had not adopted bylaws, operating rules, or participant-access terms, and involved founders representing roughly one-half of worldwide auto production. The FTC reserved the right to act if the public interest required it. This supports a narrow lesson: **governance, access rules, and actual operations are part of the product—not paperwork to postpone after a coalition has announced itself.** It does not establish that every industry utility requires the same legal analysis. [4]

### Direct verification note: XML 1.0

W3C's February 10, 1998 XML 1.0 Recommendation states that XML was designed to be straightforwardly usable over the Internet, support a wide variety of applications, be compatible with SGML, be easy to process and create, minimize optional features, remain human-legible, and be formal and concise. The document had been reviewed by W3C Members and interested parties and was endorsed as a stable recommendation intended to enhance Web functionality and interoperability. This provides a valuable design discipline for the Human Blockchain vocabulary layer: **human-readable structure, a small mandatory core, extensibility at the edges, and clear conformance.** It does not justify adopting XML itself as the present technical format or assuming that an open specification automatically creates adoption. ## Provisional Human Blockchain interpretation

The evidence supports a **layered institutional design**, not a single all-powerful market hub: a governed vocabulary and identity/evidence substrate; a multi-stakeholder contribution and decision process; and separate commercial/operational applications. It does **not** support prematurely presenting Human Blockchain as a universal exchange, an investment vehicle, a mandated industry standard, or a replacement for sector-specific systems.
