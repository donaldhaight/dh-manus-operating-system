## Provisional Human Blockchain interpretation

The current cycle makes one limit clear: **scale infrastructure can accelerate computation, but it does not automatically create legitimate coordination**. Human Blockchain’s proposed value remains a different and smaller task—making evidence, role authority, decision rights, dissent, and learning inspectable within a narrow real-world workflow. It should use AI as a bounded capability inside that workflow, not imitate the capital intensity, political claims, or centralization of the compute race.

## Direct verification notes

### The Genesis Mission: official title and mandate

The official White House source is Executive Order **14363**, titled **“Launching the Genesis Mission,”** dated November 24, 2025. The order establishes the **Genesis Mission** as a national effort to accelerate the application of AI for transformative scientific discovery. It assigns implementation within the Department of Energy to the Secretary of Energy and general leadership/agency coordination to the Assistant to the President for Science and Technology through the National Science and Technology Council. [1]

The order directs establishment of an **American Science and Security Platform** that may integrate DOE supercomputing and secure cloud environments, AI agents and analysis frameworks, computational tools, domain-specific foundation models, and appropriately secured federal, proprietary, and open scientific data. It expressly calls for data standardization, metadata, provenance tracking, risk-based cybersecurity, and an initial operating-capability demonstration, subject to applicable law and available appropriations. [1]

The user’s phrase **“Trump’s Genesis Mission”** is understandable descriptive shorthand because the order was issued by the Trump administration, but it is not the order’s official program name. The historic corpus will use “Genesis Mission” in formal references and will not infer operational results, permanent funding, social license, or model safety from the order alone.

> “This order launches the ‘Genesis Mission’ as a dedicated, coordinated national effort to unleash a new age of AI-accelerated innovation and discovery.” — Executive Order 14363 [1]

### OpenAI’s stated nonprofit–PBC structure

OpenAI’s current structure page says the organization was founded as a nonprofit in 2015; that its for-profit subsidiary was created in 2019; and that, under an October 28, 2025 update, the nonprofit became the OpenAI Foundation while the for-profit became OpenAI Group PBC. The page states that the Foundation controls the PBC through special voting and governance rights, appoints its board, holds 26% equity, and that Microsoft holds roughly 27% at the time described. [4]

This is a **self-description of governance and capitalization**, not independent evidence that the structure achieves its mission, distributes benefits fairly, prevents concentration, or establishes a model Human Blockchain should copy. Its useful research question is narrower: which critical rights are held by the purpose-oriented entity, and can those rights be inspected?

### Anthropic’s stated Long-Term Benefit Trust

Anthropic describes its Long-Term Benefit Trust as an independent five-trustee Delaware purpose trust with the same purpose as Anthropic. The company says the Trust must use its powers to ensure a responsible balance among stockholder financial interests, interests of people affected by Anthropic's conduct, and the company’s public-benefit purpose. It expressly characterizes the arrangement as an **experiment**, says it is not yet holding the structure out as an example to emulate, and describes amendment/failsafe provisions that seek to balance durability and flexibility. [5]

The corpus should preserve that candor. A purpose trust is a concrete allocation of some decision rights, but its effectiveness is an empirical governance question. Human Blockchain should seek testable authority, review, amendment, and exception paths in its own narrow work—not borrow a provider’s structure as branding.

### NIST Center for AI Standards and Innovation

NIST describes the Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) as the U.S. government’s primary industry contact for testing and collaborative research concerning commercial AI systems. Its stated activities include developing guidelines and best practices with NIST organizations, helping industry develop **voluntary** standards, making voluntary agreements with private-sector developers and evaluators, leading unclassified evaluations related to demonstrable national-security risks, coordinating with federal entities, and representing U.S. interests in international standards activity. [6]

This is a useful institutional differentiation: a government center can convene, measure, evaluate, coordinate, and publish voluntary standards without becoming the operating authority for every participating system. Human Blockchain should likewise be explicit about its limited authority; its internal evidence-to-decision layer is neither a regulator nor an AI safety certifier.

### NIST AI Agent Standards Initiative

NIST's AI Agent Standards Initiative, created February 17, 2026 and updated August 14, 2026, describes three strategic pillars: convening/analysis for industry-led technical standards and voluntary guidelines; engagement to reduce barriers to community-led interoperable protocols; and research into agent authentication, identity, and security evaluations. The page also identifies requests for information, a draft concept paper on software and AI-agent identity and authorization, and listening sessions as participation mechanisms. [7]

The direct lesson is not “agents are ready to act unattended.” It is that an agentic ecosystem needs interoperable protocols **and** identity, authorization, security evaluation, stakeholder input, and a visible process for revising guidance. This supports the Human Blockchain pilot’s existing emphasis on scoped tools, role authority, human gates, provenance, and audit trails.

### IEA: energy demand from AI and data centres

The International Energy Agency’s *Energy and AI* analysis states that AI-model training and deployment occur mainly in data centres and estimates their 2024 global electricity consumption at roughly 415 TWh, about 1.5% of global consumption. Its Base Case projects data-centre consumption at roughly 945 TWh by 2030, while explicitly noting material uncertainty, scenario dependence, and different build times between a data centre and broader energy infrastructure. The IEA also warns that demand is geographically concentrated, which can make grid integration more difficult even when the global share remains limited. [9]

This supports a carefully stated conclusion: compute is physical infrastructure with localized power, equipment, cooling, resilience, planning, and investment dependencies. It does **not** establish a particular facility’s water use, a universal power-shortage forecast, or the desirability of any particular energy policy.

### DOE: implementation framing of the Genesis Mission

The Department of Energy’s Genesis Mission page describes the initiative as uniting DOE National Laboratories, industry, academia, and others to use AI for energy, scientific discovery, and national-security aims. It identifies an American Science and Security Platform, a collaboration/consortium area, national challenges, and a designated DOE director, and it lists later funding, partner-commitment, project-selection, and open-model announcements. [2]

This verifies an implementation-facing public presence beyond the executive-order text. The page's “world’s most powerful” framing is DOE messaging, not an independently verified comparative metric. Likewise, its listings establish announcements and intended activity, not that the mission has already achieved all stated scientific or economic outcomes.

### ISO/IEC 42001:2023

ISO lists ISO/IEC 42001:2023, *Information technology — Artificial intelligence — Management system*, as a published international standard. ISO says the standard specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System for organizations that provide or use AI-based products or services. [8]

The relevance is procedural rather than promotional: a management-system standard offers a structured way to organize risk, responsibility, traceability, and improvement. It neither certifies a particular Human Blockchain design nor proves that any organization using the standard is safe, effective, or trustworthy.

### LF AI & Data: nonprofit host with membership and technical governance

LF AI & Data presents itself as part of the Linux Foundation project ecosystem, with governance documents, a governing board, a technical advisory council, project incubation, working groups, and different membership levels. Its join page says corporate participation requires Linux Foundation corporate membership and describes the umbrella foundation as providing legal-entity, financial, accounting, audit, and supporting resources. It distinguishes academic/nonprofit, general, and premier participation, with governance participation and board-related rights varying by level. [10]

This is a concrete example of a **foundation host plus project/community governance plus paid organizational support** model. It demonstrates the questions Human Blockchain would have to answer before claiming a coalition or association: who may contribute, who may govern, what membership pays for, which rights are technical versus commercial, and how the host remains accountable.

### Federal permitting for data-center infrastructure

Executive Order **14318**, *Accelerating Federal Permitting of Data Center Infrastructure*, dated July 23, 2025, identifies a data-center project as a facility requiring more than 100 MW of new load dedicated to AI inference, training, simulation, or synthetic-data generation. It defines qualifying projects in terms including at least $500 million in committed capital expenditures, an incremental load over 100 MW, national-security protection, or designation by named federal secretaries. The order directs agencies to consider financial support, expedited review mechanisms, and federal land/resources, and it revokes Executive Order 14141. [3]

The order is evidence that AI infrastructure has become a federal permitting and industrial-policy concern involving land, transmission, power equipment, chips, networking, storage, finance, and environmental-review processes. It does not establish that any qualifying project will be built, that review will be risk-free, or that a more rapid federal process resolves local energy, water, community, or environmental tradeoffs.
